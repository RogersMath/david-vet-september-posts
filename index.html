Segment posts: veterinary and plastic surgery

Fifteen numbered posts. Not dated, not sequenced — drop any one where it fits. Eight for veterinary audiences, seven for plastic and reconstructive surgery.

The voice these are written in

Veterinary Posts 1–8

The buying logic is chair time and client compliance. A bandage change is a visit, sometimes a sedation, and a wrestling match. All of it lands on the practice or the owner directly.

1Proud flesh and silicone

Anyone managing distal limb wounds in horses knows the pattern: the wound looks like it's closing, then exuberant granulation tissue outruns the epithelium and you're debriding again. Ducharme-Desjarlais and colleagues placed paired wounds on the metacarpi of five horses, treated half with a silicone dressing and half with a conventional one, and found the silicone dressing inhibited exuberant granulation formation. American Journal of Veterinary Research, 2005. It's a small study with a well-constructed design, and it has been cited in equine wound care reviews ever since. The practical implication is straightforward: the contact layer is a variable with evidence attached to it, and worth choosing deliberately.

2Why human hydrocolloids underperform in animals

A dressing that performs beautifully on a human leg ulcer can fail on a dog, and the reason isn't the dressing. The Merck Veterinary Manual states it plainly: because of differences in skin physiology, hydrocolloid dressings don't adhere well to the skin of most veterinary patients. Same product, different substrate, different result. Clinical data doesn't transfer automatically across species. Adherence and secondary bandage strategy carry more weight in veterinary practice than in any of the human trials, because the dressing usually isn't holding itself on.

3Exudate, and the wrap that defeats it

Most bandage changes veterinary teams deal with aren't scheduled. They're triggered — strikethrough, saturation, an owner calling because it smells. Foam addresses that case. Merck's wound management chapter notes foam dressings absorb substantial exudate while adding insulation and protection, and flags something easy to miss: a compressive bandage over a foam dressing can compromise the benefits the foam was chosen for. Choose for exudate volume first, then wrap in a way that preserves the choice. The sequence matters as much as the selection.

4Cost per healed wound

A business point rather than a clinical one. A $3 dressing changed daily costs more than a $9 dressing changed twice weekly. Obvious written down, rarely calculated, because the dressing has a price tag and the recheck doesn't. In veterinary medicine the recheck is the expensive half. Exam time, restraint, sometimes sedation, a technician for forty minutes, and an owner taking time off work. None of it appears on the supply invoice. If you want to run that comparison on your own caseload, send me your numbers and I'll put the model together.

5The compliance problem is a design problem

Owner compliance gets discussed as a character trait. Mostly it's a design problem. An owner told to keep a bandage dry and clean for five days, in a house with a dog who licks, will struggle for reasons unrelated to how much they love the animal. The countermeasures the profession has built — elbow pads, protective boots, external fixator covers, all standard entries in the veterinary wound care texts — exist because the difficulty is routine. The dressings that survive home care tolerate an imperfect week. Longer wear time, less pain on removal, fewer opportunities to get it wrong. Design the protocol around the household the animal is actually going home to.

6Amorphous silicate in equine limb wounds

A multicenter retrospective in JAVMA looked at horses with naturally occurring full-thickness limb lacerations treated with a topical amorphous silicate dressing. Eleven horses, eight participating US veterinarians, records from May 2021 to May 2022. It's a case series without a control arm, so it's early evidence rather than a conclusion. The proposed mechanism is that orthosilicic acid regulates fibroblast growth factor, reducing inflammation and producing more homogenous collagen and elastic fibers, which would be a plausible route to less exuberant granulation on distal limbs. Worth tracking as this line of work develops. Has anyone here used a silicate dressing on a distal limb wound?

7Stock less

Fahie and Shettko published a systematic review in Veterinary Clinics of North America on therapeutic agents to enhance granulation and epithelialization — an evidence-based sort through a long menu of options. That menu is the practical problem. Adherent, nonadherent, absorptive, semiocclusive, occlusive, moisture-retaining, alginates, hypertonic saline, hydrogels, foams. All justified for some wound. Very little of it justified for one clinic's actual caseload. Most practices can cover the large majority of what walks in with one absorbent foam, one nonadherent contact layer, one hydrogel for dry or sloughy wounds, and a secondary bandage system. Fewer stockouts, less expiry, and a protocol the staff can actually learn. How many wound care SKUs are on your shelf, and how many moved last quarter?

8Human-market supply, veterinary practice

Most advanced wound dressings used in veterinary medicine were developed, tested, and cleared for people. That's simply how the supply chain works — the human market funds the material science and veterinary practice inherits it. Two consequences worth holding onto. The clinical evidence behind a given dressing may not have been generated in your species, which is why the equine and small animal literature on specific products carries disproportionate weight. And you're buying from manufacturers whose quality systems were built to human regulatory requirements, which generally works in your favor. The useful conversation with any supplier starts there: what does the evidence base actually cover, and what does your practice have to figure out on its own?

Plastic and reconstructive surgery Posts 9–15

Cash-pay and outcome-driven. The scar is what the patient paid for. Unit cost barely matters; revision rates and photographs do.

9Silicone as first line

On scar management the guidelines are unusually settled. The updated international practical guidelines, developed by a multidisciplinary group of 24 experts, name silicone sheeting or gel as the first-line prophylactic and treatment option for hypertrophic scars and keloids. The earlier international recommendations landed in the same place, concluding that silicone gel sheeting and intralesional corticosteroids were the only treatments with evidence sufficient to support an evidence-based recommendation. That level of agreement is rare in wound care, and it moves the real question downstream: how do you get a patient to use it for the months it takes?

10Sheet or gel

The sheet-versus-gel question usually gets argued on efficacy. The evidence suggests that's the wrong axis. Comparative work has found fluid silicone gels at least equivalent to sheets, with a prospective comparison showing similar Vancouver Scar Scale outcomes at one to three months. The difference shows up in adherence — gel is reported as more convenient, particularly at mobile or exposed sites and in warm climates. Sheets carry their own friction. Not fully self-adhering without a covering or clothing to hold them. They collect dirt and perspiration. In humid conditions patients can develop heat rash underneath. And the original work suggested at least 12 hours of daily wear to be effective. In a humid climate, format is a clinical decision rather than a preference.

11Compliance is the active ingredient

A 2024 paper from the plastic surgery department at Kyung Hee University Hospital examined something most scar research skips: whether patients actually used the silicone they were given, and whether that tracked with results. It did. Higher compliance with silicone gel sheeting and topical gel was associated with better scar outcomes. The finding is intuitive and still useful, because it moves the variable from the product to the handoff. If a first-line therapy works at 12-plus hours a day across months, then the counseling, the packaging, the reminder, and the follow-up appointment are part of the treatment. The best product you can dispense is the one still being used in week ten.

12Donor sites

The split-thickness graft donor site is the wound nobody optimizes. Routine, iatrogenic, often large, and frequently the thing the patient complains about most. Two things drive the complaint, and both are addressable. Exudate volume is high early, so absorption capacity and wear time determine how often anyone has to touch the wound. And in the surgical literature, dressing removal is named as a well-known trigger of acute pain, because adherence to the wound bed or dried exudate causes real discomfort — with pain control documented as a major factor in how surgeons choose a donor site dressing. The donor site gets managed with whatever the OR stocks. It's worth a deliberate protocol of its own.

13How long should the dressing stay on?

CDC's guideline for the prevention of surgical site infection recommends protecting a primarily closed incision with a sterile dressing for 24 to 48 hours. Past 48 hours there's no recommendation — not a recommendation against, an absence of one. So for most elective plastic surgery, dressing wear time is set by individual surgeon experience. Trials in breast augmentation and breast reconstruction have been designed specifically because investigators describe the existing standards as empiric and scarce. The practical response isn't to wait for the literature. A practice with a written, consistent post-op dressing protocol is already ahead of a field running mostly on inherited habit — and a written protocol is the only kind you can evaluate and improve.

14The scar is the deliverable

In elective aesthetic surgery the patient didn't buy a procedure. They bought a result they'll see in a mirror for thirty years. The scar literature is direct about the downside: hypertrophic scars and keloids can be aesthetically displeasing and produce severe psychosocial impairment. That's the bad outcome for someone who paid out of pocket and chose a surgeon from before-and-after photographs. Which makes the dressing and the scar protocol the last mile of the outcome the practice markets on — the part that happens after the surgical skill is already spent.

15Build the take-home kit

A suggestion for practices running volume in elective cases: stop handing out scar care as an afterthought at the two-week visit. The guidelines call for silicone started early and continued as long as necessary, with re-evaluation every four to eight weeks. That's a months-long protocol often delivered in a thirty-second doorway conversation. Package it instead. Silicone, written instructions with a start date on them, sun protection guidance, and the re-evaluation appointment booked before the patient leaves. A few dollars of supply and ten minutes of staff training. Patients read it as thoroughness, and it's among the cheapest interventions available against a revision.
